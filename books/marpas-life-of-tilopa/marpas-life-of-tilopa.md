## 0 Homage, and the three jewels

*Namo guru deva ḍākinī. Samayam anusmara. Siddhyantu ye.*[^invocation]

From the three bodies[^three-bodies], beyond elaboration[^beyond-elaboration] and wide as the sky, that ripen those to be trained[^to-be-trained] in the nine moods of the dance[^nine-moods], the three bodies like a great lake, unstirred and a little clear, whose waves are the ḍākinīs[^dakini], came the lotus-born[^lotus-born], great bliss[^great-bliss], the fruit[^fruit] itself made manifest, Prajñābhadra[^prajnabhadra], protector of beings[^protector-of-beings]. To him, and to the great paṇḍita[^pandita] Nāropa, who bore the twelve great hardships[^twelve-hardships], I bow.

> The jewel of the three bodies,\
> sealed against all who do not know it,\
> with the key, the ḍākinīs' secret word[^secret-word],\
> the three jewels[^three-jewels] are shown in full:\
> by the wish-fulfilling jewel[^wish-fulfilling-jewel] of introduction[^introduction]\
> the dharma body[^dharma-body] is pointed out;\
> by the wish-fulfilling jewel of dream[^dream]\
> the enjoyment body[^enjoyment-body] is made to dwell in the body;\
> by the wish-fulfilling jewel of the lineage[^lineage]\
> the dharma body is shown as it is.\
> The ḍākinīs' whispered lineage[^whispered-lineage], the clear light[^clear-light] of the sun,\
> dreads to hear Rāhu the titan[^titan] named;\
> yet for Dode's sake I have set it down in letters:\
> ḍākinīs, forgive me, and grant your blessing[^blessing].\
> The whispered lineage's instructions[^instruction] are not shown to all:\
> hungry, still one does not eat one's father's and mother's flesh;\
> for gain, still one does not sell a deadly poison;\
> for a great show, still one does not put one's own heart on display.

This wondrous whispered lineage of the ḍākinīs has three parts: the marks of the master who teaches, the marks of the disciple who is taught, and the nature of the Dharma[^dharma] that is taught. The first: a master of the lineage, with experience and realization[^experience-realization], and with compassion[^compassion], and the rest. The second: a disciple able to seek buddhahood[^buddhahood] in his own mind in this one life and this one body[^one-life], and the rest. The third, the nature of the Dharma that is taught, is in three: outwardly, the instruction of the emanation body[^emanation-body], which clears away false notions[^false-notions], the wish-fulfilling jewel of the lineage; inwardly, the instruction of the enjoyment body, practiced in experience, the wish-fulfilling jewel of the path of ripening[^path-of-ripening]; and secretly[^secretly], the instruction of the dharma body, which points out, the wish-fulfilling jewel of the path of freedom[^path-of-freedom].

Of these, this is the instruction of the emanation body, the wish-fulfilling jewel of the lineage. It holds two accounts: the account of the wisdom ḍākinī[^wisdom-dakini] of the dharma body, and the account of the lineage of the accomplished ones[^accomplished-ones] of the emanation body. The first: as all the buddhas dwell in three bodies, so this wisdom ḍākinī dwells in three bodies, as is told at length below. The second, the lives of the lineage of the accomplished ones of the emanation body, begins with the life of Tilo, and it has four parts: how he was known as a man; how he was known as an emanation[^emanation] of Cakrasaṃvara[^cakrasamvara]; how he was known as Cakrasaṃvara himself; and how he was known as the body in which all the buddhas gather.

## 1 Tilopa as a man

First, taken as a man, the great lord Tilopa's great qualities are four: first, in the provisional sense[^provisional], how the ḍākinīs foretold him, and he sought a guru[^guru] and practiced; how he overwhelmed the ḍākinīs and asked them for the Dharma; how, in the definitive sense[^definitive], he was shown to have no human guru; and how he showed his manifold emanations.

## 1.1 The boy who herded buffalo

First, Tilopa. In the east of India, in the land of Zahor, in a city called Dzako, there lived three: the father, the Brahmin[^brahmin] Salwa; the mother, the Brahmin Salden; and the sister, the Brahmin Saldrön. They had no son, and when they had made offerings to every shrine, outer and inner, and prayed, a son was born. At that moment the whole east of India was filled with light, so he was named Salö, Clear Light. Then they asked a Brahmin who read the marks[^read-the-marks], and he said:

> Whether he is a god[^god], a nāga[^naga] or a yakṣa[^yaksa],\
> or a buddha, I cannot tell:\
> cherish this holy being and guard him.

As he said, they cherished him and guarded him. Once, when the father had gone out and mother and son were alone, something flickered, and when they looked, there were many ugly women with the marks[^with-the-marks], puffing out their breath, limping and leaning on sticks. The mother thought, "Are they demons[^demon]? Will my boy die?" and the women said:

> Cherish him and guard him as you may,\
> there is no land on earth where none must die.

So they said. His mother asked, "Then what can we do that will help?" and they said again:

> Let the boy herd buffalo and learn his letters:\
> a ḍākinī's prophecy[^prophecy] will come.

With that, they say, they vanished without a trace.

Then, when he had grown up and they had set him to do so, he was herding the buffalo when the women from before came again and asked about his country, his father and mother and the rest, and the boy said:

> My country is Dzako, in the east;\
> my father is Salwa, a Brahmin;\
> my mother is Salden, a Brahmin;\
> my sister is Saldrön, a Brahmin;\
> and I myself am Salö, a Brahmin.\
> This tree is a sal tree.[^sal]\
> I have learned my letters, and I practice the holy Dharma;\
> I herd the buffalo for our wealth.

When he said this, the women, as though they were angry, said, "Child, you do not know:

> your country is Oḍḍiyāna[^oddiyana], in the west;\
> your father is Cakrasaṃvara;\
> your mother is Vajravārāhī[^vajravarahi];\
> your sister is the ḍākinī Giver of Bliss;\
> you yourself are Pañcapana.[^pancapana]\
> Do not herd the beast, the buffalo:\
> in the forest of the tree of awakening[^tree-of-awakening]\
> herd always the buffalo of experience."

So they said. The boy said, "I do not know how to herd that," and they said, "Go to the charnel ground[^charnel-ground] of Sala Bhehara. A guru will show you." So they foretold.

In the southern lands there is a charnel ground called Sala Bhehara, blessed by Maheśvara[^mahesvara]. When the outer and inner ḍākinīs were turning the wheel of the feast[^feast] there, the Brahmin's boy came, and he asked Caryāpa[^caryapa] for instruction too.

The great master Lawapa[^lawapa] sang:

> At the crossroads of a great city\
> I slept for twelve years\
> and won the siddhi[^siddhi] of Mahāmudrā[^mahamudra].

When he said this, the boy asked him for luminosity[^luminosity].

He looked for Nāgārjuna[^nagarjuna]. In that charnel ground, in an outcaste's[^outcaste] hut, a yogin[^yogin] who kept the yogin's discipline[^discipline] sat meditating. "Nāgārjuna has gone to teach the Dharma to the king of the gandharvas[^gandharva]," he said. "I have been sent to take you into my care, good man." The boy offered him a maṇḍala[^mandala] and asked, and he called up before him the maṇḍala of glorious Guhyasamāja[^guhyasamaja], gave him its empowerment[^empowerment] and explained its tantra[^tantra], and the boy saw the nature of mind[^nature-of-mind].

In a temple there, where his uncle was reciting the Perfection of Wisdom in a Hundred Thousand Lines, a woman came and said, "Do you know what this means?" He said he did not. "Then I will explain it," she said, and she explained its meaning. She gave him the empowerments of Hevajra[^hevajra] and Cakrasaṃvara and explained their tantras. "This is what Lūhipa[^luhipa] meant," she said. "Meditate on it." When he said, "My uncle keeps me at the Hundred Thousand and will not let me meditate," she said, "Cut the cord that binds the Hundred Thousand, throw it from the temple door into the water, put on the guise of a madman and meditate. I will bless it, and nothing at all will befall the Hundred Thousand." He did as she said, and nothing at all befell the Hundred Thousand.[^hundred] "He has gone mad," they said, and they locked him up. There he trained in the two stages[^two-stages], the essence of all the paths that shine clearly in the undivided mind of the Sugatas[^sugata].

Then came a prophecy:

> In the east, in the land of Bengal,\
> in the market of Pañcapana,\
> become the servant of Bharima the harlot\
> and her household, and you will be made clean:\
> bring the conduct[^conduct] to its end, and win accomplishment[^accomplishment].

So it was foretold, and he went there. By night he did the work of bringing men in and seeing them out; by day he did the work of pounding sesame, so that in the language of India he was known as Tilopa, and in Tibetan as Til Sungzhab, the Sesame Keeper.

Then he went to the charnel ground called Kereli, where he and Bharima delighted each other[^delighted] in the conduct of the secret mantra[^secret-mantra], and brought it to its end.

Then, back in that market, as he went on pounding sesame, he won the accomplishment that is close to the supreme Mahāmudrā. The people of the city saw him in different ways: some saw a blazing mass of fire, and some saw fire blazing in his bone ornaments[^bone-ornaments]. They asked him for instruction, and he said, "Let the innate[^innate] that has arisen in my mind enter the hearts of you who follow me," and at once they were freed[^freed].

Then the king of that country came riding an elephant with his court to honor them, and the Brahmin's boy and Bharima, the two of them, sang a vajra song[^vajra-song] in a great voice like Brahmā's[^brahma]:

> Sesame oil, the essence that lies within it:\
> the dull may know that it is in the seed,\
> but if they do not know the links of dependent arising[^dependent-arising]\
> they cannot bring the essence, the oil, out.\
> Just so the innate, primordial wisdom[^primordial-wisdom],\
> though it is in the heart of every being,\
> cannot be realized unless the guru points it out.\
> When the sesame is pounded and the husk is cleared away,\
> the essence, the oil, comes out; just so,\
> relying on the guru, the meaning of suchness[^suchness]\
> is shown in signs[^signs], as the oil of the sesame is.\
> All things become one nature, not divided.\
> Kye ho[^kye-ho]! The meaning far off and hard to fathom\
> is clear right now. What a wonder!

When they had sung this, all who were gathered there were freed. The meaning of the song, untied: there is nothing out of keeping with the meaning to give up, no remedy[^remedy], wisdom, to bring about, no grounds and paths[^grounds-paths] to travel, no fruit to gain; only as a convention[^convention], in signs, must the guru show it. That is what it means. Then they asked him who his guru was, and he said:

> I have human gurus:\
> Nāgārjuna, Caryāpa, Lawapa,\
> and Kalpa Zangmo; these are mine,\
> the gurus of the four transmissions[^four-transmissions].

So he said. From Nāgārjuna he asked for the illusory body[^illusory-body], from Caryāpa for dream, from Lawapa for luminosity, and from the ḍākinī for inner heat[^inner-heat]; and these are known as the four great rivers of the whispered lineage.

## 1.2 The ḍākinīs brought under his power, and the jewels asked for

Second, how he brought the ḍākinīs under his power and asked them for the Dharma. Again the women came and said:

> The whispered lineage without letters\
> is kept by the stainless ḍākinīs:\
> receive the three wish-fulfilling jewels.

When they said this, he said, "I cannot receive them," and again the women said:

> At Gandhola in the land of Oḍḍiyāna\
> one foretold and true to the pledge[^pledge],\
> one with the fortune[^fortune] to take them, receives them.

When they said this, he asked what he must do to receive them, and they told him, "Take a crystal ladder, a bridge of jewels and a pair of grass mice, and go to Oḍḍiyāna."[^ladder] His father found these without trouble, and he took them and went.

Now in the land of Oḍḍiyāna in the west, in the temple of Gandhola, dwells the one called the ḍākinī of wisdom, the dharma body: in the state where reference[^reference] has ceased, in non-duality[^non-duality], dwelling in the manner of not dwelling, she dwells as a queen. Beside her is the one called the ḍākinī of the five families[^five-families], the enjoyment body: in a palace of jewels she keeps the three precious wish-fulfilling jewels hidden, locked in with indestructible iron and sealed with seven seals, and ringed by a fortress, a moat and an iron wall, she dwells as a minister. Beside her is the emanation body, the ḍākinī of action[^dakini-of-action], the devourer: to those with faith and devotion[^devotion] she gives siddhis, and those without faith, and those whose pledges are broken, she cuts down and devours; she dwells as a gatekeeper. The Brahmin's boy went to the temple of Gandhola in the land of Oḍḍiyāna in the west, and the emanation body, the ḍākinī of action, came at him with a voice to crack the earth, a voice like thunder in the sky:

> I am the ḍākinī of action, the emanation body:\
> I delight in human flesh and lust for blood.

So saying, she came at him, and the Brahmin's boy said:

> Many ḍākinīs, most terrible,\
> cannot stir the tip of one hair of mine.

So he said, and he overwhelmed them with the discipline of awareness[^discipline-of-awareness]. His body did not move, his speech did not falter, his mind did not shrink, and he gazed at them; and the ḍākinīs swooned. When they came round from the swoon, they said:

> Kye ma[^kye-ma]! As the moth is destroyed by the lamp,\
> wanting to eat you, we are undone.\
> Holy one, do with us as you please.

So they said. Then the Brahmin's boy said, "Let me in," and the ḍākinīs said:

> We are like servants and have little power;\
> unless we have asked the ministers,\
> we are eaters of flesh and drinkers of blood:\
> holy one, take this to heart.

They handed him over to the ḍākinīs of the enjoyment body. These said, "Your discipline looks slight. We will set you free: go up." The Brahmin's boy laid the bridge of jewels across the moat, set the crystal ladder against the iron wall, opened the gate with the pair of grass mice and went in, and the ministers said:

> Terrible in body, terrible in speech,\
> bearing terrible weapons as well,\
> we are the ḍākinīs of the five families, the enjoyment body:\
> we delight in flesh and lust for blood.

So they said, and again the Brahmin's boy said:

> Many ḍākinīs, most terrible,\
> cannot stir one pore of my body's hair.

So he said, and he gazed at them, and they swooned. "Let me in," he said, and the ministers said:

> We are like ministers and have little power;\
> unless we have asked the queen herself,\
> we are cut down by her decree:\
> holy one, take this to heart.

So they said, and they prayed to the ḍākinī of the dharma body, and the boy went in. The wisdom ḍākinī of the dharma body sat ringed by heroes[^heroes] past counting in a row on her right and heroines in a row on her left, and the Brahmin's boy did not bow to her, and her retinue said:

> This one, before her who of all the buddhas\
> is the mother, the Blessed Lady[^blessed-lady],\
> shows no respect: why not crush him?

So saying, they set about destroying him, and then the ḍākinī of the dharma body spoke to her retinue:

> This one, of all the buddhas\
> the father, is Cakrasaṃvara:\
> though you rained vajras down from the sky,\
> how could you overcome him now?

So she said, and the retinue, as though they did not know the boy was Cakrasaṃvara, said, "Who sent you? Who are you? What do you want?" and the Brahmin's boy said:

> I am Pañcapana;\
> my sister, the Giver of Bliss, has sent me;\
> with view[^view], conduct, fruit and pledge\
> I have come to take the jewels of the three bodies.

When he said this, the retinue laughed him to scorn and said with one voice:

> The blind look and see no form,\
> the deaf listen and hear no sound,\
> the mute speak and no one grasps the meaning:\
> deceived by a demon, there is no truth in it.

To this the master answered:

> One whose faults are spent\
> has no cause to speak false words:\
> no demon, it is the ḍākinī, truly.

When he said this, the wisdom ḍākinī gave the signs of the precious jewel: of her body, a painted card[^painted-card]; of her speech, a seed syllable[^seed-syllable]; of her mind, a hand emblem[^hand-emblem]. And the Brahmin's boy said:

> From the secret treasury of the body, appearance and emptiness[^emptiness],\
> I ask for the common wish-fulfilling jewel;\
> from the secret treasury of speech, beyond words,\
> I ask for the wish-fulfilling jewel of the pledge;\
> from the secret treasury of mind, free of thought[^free-of-thought],\
> I ask for the wish-fulfilling jewel of the way things are[^way-things-are].

When he said this, the wisdom ḍākinī said:

> For the common wish-fulfilling jewel\
> the foretold key of experience is needed:\
> unforetold, it will not open.\
> For the wish-fulfilling jewel of the pledge\
> the key of the profound lineage of the word is needed:\
> without the remedy, it will not open.\
> For the wish-fulfilling jewel of the way things are\
> the key of profound wisdom is needed:\
> without realization it will not open.

So she said, and the Brahmin's boy said:

> The ḍākinīs' secret word is sealed by the heart:\
> the lamp of wisdom that clears the dark of ignorance[^ignorance],\
> self-aware[^self-aware], self-arisen, self-illumined:\
> I have the foretold key of experience.\
> In mind itself[^mind-itself], which nowhere arises,\
> self-freed[^self-freed], the dharma body,\
> self-freed, it dawns as Mahāmudrā:\
> I have the key of the pledge, self-freed.\
> Taking no object to mind,\
> no mote of thought arising,\
> in mind itself, the nature of things[^nature-of-things], the dharma body,\
> I have the key of experience that sees.

So he said, and the wisdom ḍākinīs of the enjoyment body and the emanation body, both together, sang this song with one voice:

> Our one father, the Blessed One[^blessed-one],\
> Tilo, the buddha, protector of beings,\
> Cakrasaṃvara, great bliss:\
> to you we offer the three wish-fulfilling jewels.

So saying, they gave him the root tantra[^root-tantra] in fifty-one chapters with its explanatory tantras, and the whispered lineage. And from the mouth of the ḍākinī of wisdom came this: "If you would accomplish my body, strive in the stage of generation[^generation]; my speech, in the essence[^essence]; my mind, in the stage of completion[^completion], Mahāmudrā. Go to the monastery of the Crown Jewel, and take Nāro, Riri and Kasori into your care."

With that the chief lady vanished. He was given the name Tilo Prajñābhadra, and he said:

> Like a bird in the sky,\
> the bird of luminous mind takes wing:\
> Prajñābhadra goes, unhindered.

When he said this, the ḍākinīs of the enjoyment body and the emanation body said:

> Holy one, what are you like?\
> We beg you to stay for our sake.

When they asked this, Tilo said:

> As the chief lady herself foretold,\
> for the sake of worthy vessels[^worthy-vessels], I, the yogin,\
> go to the monastery of the Crown Jewel.

So he said, and on the road as he went, the nine cycles[^nine-cycles] of the teaching of the bodiless ḍākinīs were given him out of the sky:

> The vajra speech of the bodiless ḍākinīs,\
> like the sweet songs of the gandharvas,\
> was heard thus from the empty sky:\
> Ripening and freeing, undo the knot of mind.\
> The pledge: look into the mirror of your own mind.\
> Turn the wheel, the lattice of channels and winds[^channels-winds].\
> Hold the precious jewel of great bliss, of speech.\
> Look at the lamp of awareness[^awareness], of wisdom.\
> Look at self-freed Mahāmudrā.\
> The sacred substance[^sacred-substance]: the sun of realization, the key.\
> Conduct: strike the water with a sword.\
> One taste[^one-taste]: look into the outer mirror.

He heard these from the sky, and Tilo said:

> As the chief lady showed the way,\
> in Gandhola, the illusory body,\
> I have taken in the secret of the bodiless ḍākinīs\
> and locked it with speech beyond words:\
> the bird of luminous mind flies off.

So saying, he went to the temple of Aḍona, the monastery of the Crown Jewel.

## 1.3 No human guru

Third, that he had no human guru. Many people gathered there, and since he had gained accomplishment and received the Dharma at Gandhola, they asked him who his guru was:

> I have no human guru:\
> my guru is the All-Knowing[^all-knowing].

So he said.

## 1.4 The eight tamings

Fourth, how he showed his manifold emanations. There are eight: he overwhelmed the yogin, and he tamed the heretic[^heretic], the magician, the beer-seller, the singer, the butcher, the one who says acts bear no fruit[^acts-no-fruit], and the great sorcerer.[^order]

### 1.4.1 The yogin

First. In the south of India there was a king who loved his mother dearly and did whatever pleased her. "What good deed would please you?" he asked her. "I will do it." His mother said, "I would be glad if you gathered paṇḍitas, siddhas[^siddha] and ḍākinīs, raised a maṇḍala of jewels in the sky, and held a great empowerment and a feast." So he sent a runner to each of the paṇḍitas who could do it, and one runner to the siddhas, and invited the paṇḍitas and the siddhas. The paṇḍitas performed the rite of the ground[^rite-of-the-ground] and the rest, and it was proclaimed that whatever these did would succeed. Then an ugly woman with the marks came before the kusālis[^kusali] and said, "Who will be the master of your feast?" The Unrivaled said, "I will." "You will not do," she said. "My brother will." When they asked where he was, she said, "In the charnel ground." "Then go and fetch him," they said, and she went to fetch him and said that he would come. At the very end he came, and he sat with the Unrivaled on two thrones. They debated the logic that shows the contradictions and connections of word and meaning, and they were equal. They drew maṇḍalas in the sky and took turns making them vanish with rain and wind, and they were equal. They rode lions in a race to the sun and the moon, and again they were equal. Then Tilo brought the sun and the moon down to the ground and rode a lion on them; he turned his body inside out, and on each hair he called up a maṇḍala with its charnel ground, and in each charnel ground a tree, and in each tree he sat cross-legged at play; and the Unrivaled could not do it. All were amazed. "E ma[^e-ma]! From what does such magic come?

> From what does such wondrous conduct come?"

When they said this, he answered in song:

> Kye ma! When I sit, I sit in the midst of the sky;\
> when I lie down, I lie on the point of a spear;\
> when I look, I look into the heart of the sun and moon.\
> I am Tilopa, who has realized the ultimate[^ultimate]:\
> I am free of all striving.

So he said, and when he spoke of the nature beyond thought[^beyond-thought], all were freed. The yogin was given the name Mighty Intellect, and even now he lives in Oḍḍiyāna without passing away[^without-passing-away].

### 1.4.2 The heretic

Second, the taming of the heretic. At glorious Śrī Nālandā[^nalanda] there was a heretic teacher, a siddha, before whom all, outsiders and insiders[^outsiders-insiders], had always to rise; whoever did not had to vie with him in power and in debate. Tilopa did not rise, and he said, "You seem to think yourself something in power and in debate. Let us vie." They set the king as judge, and all the paṇḍitas, outsiders and insiders, gathered on the terms that whoever lost would enter the teaching of the one who won. In debate the heretic was defeated. Vying in power, he could not prevail against Tilopa either, and the heretic sent fire out of his mouth and said:

> I who shake all three realms[^three-realms],\
> who is there that does not fear me?

When he said this, Tilopa turned it back and answered in song:

> If you would look, look with a blind man's eyes;\
> if you thirst, drink the water of a mirage;\
> if you sink, fill the vase[^vase] with wind:\
> Tilo is free of every fear.

So he said, and when he spoke of the nature of things beyond thought, all were freed. The heretic was given the name Black Virtue, and even now he lives in the Cool Grove[^cool-grove] without passing away.

### 1.4.3 The magician

Third, the taming of the magician. A magician was taking the king's country with an army of illusion. An ugly woman with the marks came and asked the king, "Who will be your general?" "This one and that one," he said. "They will not do," she said. "My brother will." "Where is your brother?" "In the charnel ground, on a sal tree a league high, he has tied a horse's tail; to it he has tied a corpse's legs; to its arms he has tied five horses; and he holds on to them and dances." So she said. When they told the king, he said, "That woman has spoken of three things that cannot be. Bring her here," and when they asked her, she said just what she had said before. He sent men to look, and it was as she had said. Then they invited Tilopa. Not knowing the army for an illusion, they were on the point of being routed, when he broke the illusion, killed some, and seized the magician. The magician said:

> You, a man who relies on the Dharma,\
> how can it be right to kill like that?

When he said this, Tilopa answered in song:

> In killing an army of illusion,\
> since it has no mind, there is no sin[^sin].\
> If you would kill, kill the man of illusion[^man-of-illusion];\
> if you would meditate, meditate as one without breath;\
> if you would speak, speak with a mute's tongue;\
> if you would desire, take the letters to their end.

So he said, and when he spoke of things beyond thought, all were freed. The magician was given the name Deceiver Who Tells the Truth, and even now he lives at Aṭṭahāsa[^attahasa] without passing away.[^magician]

### 1.4.4 The beer-seller

Fourth, the taming of the beer-seller. Once, when a woman who sold beer was selling her beer, a monkey and a cat that Tilo had sent forth drank her beer on credit, and she wept. People asked what was wrong, and she told them the tale. They said, "Pray to that yogin himself," and the beer-seller wept and said:

> My living is cut off:\
> in your compassion, take me into your care.

When she said this, in an instant he filled every jar of beer with beer, and sang a song:

> If you thirst, drink the boiling water of poison;\
> if you stray, chase the monkey and kill it;\
> for a remedy, rely on the great meditator, the cat;\
> turn everything into the one taste of the innate.\
> I am Tilopa, beggar of the ultimate,\
> a yogin you have never seen.

So he said, and when he spoke of great bliss beyond thought, all were freed. The beer-seller was given the name Sunlight Lamp, and even now she lives on the island of Sosa without passing away.[^ifyou]

### 1.4.5 The singer

Fifth, the taming of the singer. There was a skilled singer. Before him Tilopa sang a song of his own making. "Will you compete?" the singer asked. "Let us compete," he said, and the singer said, "Master, take up a song." "I will sing after," said Tilopa. The singer sang his song through to its end; then the master sang, and his song had no end, and the singer could not match him. He said:

> I am a singer who puts even\
> the world of Brahmā to shame.

When he said this, Tilopa answered in song:

> If you grieve, look at the city of the gandharvas;\
> if you listen, listen to the song of the bees;\
> if you look, look with a blind man's eyes:\
> all that is heard is like an echo.

So he said, and when he spoke of music beyond thought, all were freed. The singer was given the name Melodious Mute, and even now he lives in Nagara without passing away.[^singer]

### 1.4.6 The butcher

Sixth, the taming of the butcher. A butcher, who had killed all the young of living beings, was raising one son of his own. When he had cooked the meat and opened the carcass, meaning to give it to his son, Tilo made it appear to him as his own son's legs and arms. The butcher said:

> The sin I have done myself,\
> has it come round on me, or what is this?

He was tormented with grief, and Tilopa said, "Then if you kill no others, your own son will come back." "I will not kill," he said, and Tilopa answered in song:

> If you would open, open the heart of the clear oil;\
> if you would burn, burn with the fire at the end of time[^fire-end-of-time];\
> if you would draw out, draw the wind-mind[^wind-mind] out through the crown[^crown];\
> if you would wash, wash away the stains of mind.

So he said, and when he spoke of the practice of killing beyond thought, all were freed. The butcher was given the name Bliss-Making Joy, and even now he lives on the island of the rākṣasas[^raksasa] without passing away.[^butcher]

### 1.4.7 The one who said acts bear no fruit

Seventh, the taming of the one who held that acts bear no fruit. A Cārvāka[^carvaka], who denied cause and fruit, said there is neither virtue[^virtue] nor sin; the Buddhists said there is; and they made the master the judge of their debate. The master said, "Those who hold that acts have causes and fruits win." The Cārvāka said, "No one has seen it with his eyes." So the master led him off and showed him, by emanation, the gods and the hells[^hell] and all the rest. In one heaven there was a goddess without a mate, and when he asked what she was, they said, "Some heretic is doing virtue; she is to be his companion." Then he led him to hell, and in every copper cauldron something was boiling; one had nothing in it, and when he asked what was to be boiled there, they said, "All the heretics who say there is neither virtue nor sin are boiled here," and he was terrified, they say. He said:

> By the sin heaped up by acts,\
> hell circles in one's own mind;\
> by the virtue heaped up by acts,\
> the higher realms[^higher-realms] circle in one's own mind.

When he said this, the master answered in song:

> If you cling, go off to the island of charnel grounds;\
> if you are low, turn to the top of the victory banner[^victory-banner].\
> Discursive thought[^discursive-thought] is the emanation body:\
> I have nothing at all to teach.

So he said, and when he spoke of the manifold beyond thought, all were freed. The Cārvāka was given the name Jinabodhi, and even now he lives on Śrīparvata[^sriparvata] without passing away.

Besides these, by the bearing of his body he taught the Dharma to sinners: to a craftsman as a craftsman skilled in craft, to a meditator as one accomplished, and so to fishermen and dog keepers and others without number. They told him that they were fishermen and the like, and when they had told him, they said:

> Tilopa, who has realized the ultimate,\
> for you there is not even a name for virtue and sin.

So they said, and they asked him for instruction.

### 1.4.8 The sorcerer

Eighth, the taming of the sorcerer. A sorcerer had been killing everyone with his black magic, and Tilo saw that the time had come to tame him. The two vied in cutting down, and they were equal; but of all who died, Tilo took up the dead, and some, the women and others, he did not take. He made the sorcerer himself like an old tree stump, and said, "Now, if I raise your women up, will you give up your magic?" The sorcerer could not bear it and said:[^sorcerer]

> This conduct, from which no one recovers,\
> is it any different from a butcher's?

When he said this, Tilopa answered in song:

> Look, look into your own mind:\
> swallow the mountain of appearances in one gulp;\
> drink the great ocean down in one draught;\
> cut the tether of saṃsāra[^samsara] and let it lie.

So he said, and when he spoke of activity beyond thought, all were freed. The sorcerer was given the name Nyiimi, and even now he lives at Kimitsikili.

## 2 An emanation of Cakrasaṃvara

The second general part: how he was shown to be an emanation of Cakrasaṃvara. In the east of India, on the bank of the Dukata, at the source of the river Khasu, in the charnel ground of the Masha Grove, there is a monastery called Sorrowless. There Tilopa's uncle and his mother, who was a nun, were his abbot and master[^abbot-master], and there he was ordained as a monk and named Kalapa. The others gave themselves to the three wheels[^three-wheels] of a monk's work, but he did not keep the Dharma: he killed many small birds and showed his head one way and his body another, and everyone mocked him. Now those in charge of discipline took counsel, and the provost[^provost], with the followers of the Dharma in general and the monks in particular, called an assembly of the Sorrowless monastery and brought him to account, and the king of that country said:[^birds]

> You, in a monk's form, killing living things:\
> what is your country, who are your abbot and master?

When he asked this, Tilopa answered in song:

> Sorrowless is my monastery;\
> my abbot and master are my mother and my uncle;\
> I myself am Tilo, the monk.\
> For ten million kalpas[^kalpa] I\
> have gone to a hundred buddha fields[^buddha-fields];\
> with Nāgārjuna and Āryadeva[^aryadeva]\
> and with the buddhas I have talked;\
> I have seen a thousand buddhas[^thousand-buddhas];\
> I have killed no living thing.

When he said this, the birds, they say, flew up with a whir and were gone, and all believed, and he was known as an emanation of Cakrasaṃvara.

## 3 Cakrasaṃvara himself

Third, how he was held to be Cakrasaṃvara himself. He went begging for alms in the east of India: going out he set his feet down slowly, his eyes looked no farther than a yoke's length[^yoke] ahead, and he recited verses of poetry; coming back he spoke the blessings of giving and receiving[^giving-receiving]; and the people grew devoted to him. The king of that country invited them all and honored them, and the king said, "Let each of you bring forth a verse on giving and receiving that does not go against the grammar of the grammarians, nor against logic, scripture, instruction, experience, or the realization of those who have realized." They all did so, and each agreed with one side or two. When his turn came the master spoke, and it went against none of them and reached the final meaning of each, and the king wrote it down:

> I have no father or mother:\
> Cakrasaṃvara, the supreme of bliss.\
> I have no abbot or master:\
> I am a self-arisen buddha.\
> I have no grammar or logic:\
> reasoning breaks out of awareness by itself.\
> Saṃvara's body, speech and mind\
> and my body, speech and mind are not divided:\
> I go into great bliss.

So he said, and he was known as Cakrasaṃvara himself.

## 4 The body of all the buddhas

Fourth, how he was shown to be the body in which all the buddhas of the three times[^three-times] gather. King Siṃhacandra invited many siddhas, honored them, and asked for empowerment. Tilopa raised a maṇḍala of colored powders[^colored-powders] in the sky, and each saw it differently, and Tilo sang a song:

> My body is Hevajra,\
> my speech is Mahāmāyā[^mahamaya],\
> my mind is Cakrasaṃvara,\
> my aggregates and elements[^aggregates-elements], Guhyasamāja,\
> my limbs, the Black One, the tantra of sound,\
> my lesser limbs, Vajrabhairava[^vajrabhairava],\
> my body's hairs, the buddhas of the three times.

So he said, and Tilopa was known as the body in which all the buddhas gather.

By such deeds, at the end, he set the beings of Zahor in the east on the path of ripening and freedom, and a city of seven hundred thousand was emptied.

So ends the life of Tilopa, lord of yogins, whom even the accomplished know as an emanation body: the account of what he did by his own great qualities, told as it is and told in full.

Written down at the monastery of glorious Drowolung for my son Dode. It is complete. Maṅgalam[^mangalam].[^colophon]

## Notes

[^invocation]: The invocation is in Sanskrit, as the Tibetan writes it: "Homage to the guru, the deity, the ḍākinī. Keep the pledge in mind. May they be accomplished."

[^three-bodies]: The three bodies (Sanskrit *trikāya*, Tibetan *sku gsum*) are the three ways a buddha is present: the dharma body, awakened mind itself, without form; the enjoyment body, the body of light in which a buddha appears to beings of great realization; and the emanation body, the form in which a buddha appears in the world. Each has its own note at its first use.

[^beyond-elaboration]: Beyond elaboration (*spros bral*): free of everything the mind builds, such as existing and not existing, one and many, coming and going. It describes the dharma body and emptiness.

[^to-be-trained]: Those to be trained (*gdul bya*) are the beings a buddha or a master can bring to awakening, those a teaching is meant for.

[^nine-moods]: The nine moods of the dance are the nine moods (Sanskrit *rasa*) the deities of the tantras show as they dance: graceful, heroic and repulsive in body; laughing, fierce and terrifying in speech; compassionate, majestic and peaceful in mind.

[^dakini]: A ḍākinī (Tibetan *mkha' 'gro ma*, "she who goes in the sky") is a female being of the tantras: at the highest a buddha in female form, at the lowest a fierce spirit of the charnel grounds who eats flesh. The ḍākinīs guard the secret teachings and give them only to those fit to hold them. In this life they foretell Tilopa, test him and give him the teachings of the whispered lineage.

[^lotus-born]: "The lotus-born" is *chu skyes*, "born of water", a poet's word for the lotus: as a lotus rises from a lake, Prajñābhadra, who is Tilopa, rises from the lake of the three bodies. It is not here the name of Padmasambhava, who is often called the Lotus-Born.

[^great-bliss]: Great bliss (Sanskrit *mahāsukha*, Tibetan *bde ba chen po*) is the bliss of awakening as the tantras teach it, never apart from emptiness. It is also a name of Cakrasaṃvara, the deity of supreme bliss.

[^fruit]: The fruit (*'bras bu*) is the result of the path, awakening itself; "the fruit made manifest" calls Tilopa awakening in person. Elsewhere in the book "fruit" is also the result an act brings, as in "acts bear no fruit".

[^prajnabhadra]: Prajñābhadra, "Excellent Wisdom", is Tilopa, by the name the ḍākinīs give him in Oḍḍiyāna in 1.2.

[^protector-of-beings]: "Protector of beings" (*'gro ba'i mgon po*) is a title of a buddha, or of a master seen as one.

[^pandita]: A paṇḍita is a scholar learned in the Buddhist teachings and the sciences of India, such as the great teachers of its monastic universities.

[^twelve-hardships]: Nāropa, the great scholar who left the monastic university of Nālandā to seek Tilopa, was Tilopa's disciple and Marpa's own guru. Tradition tells of the twelve great hardships Tilopa put him through in twelve years before giving him the teachings, among them leaping from the roof of a temple at his word.

[^secret-word]: The ḍākinīs' secret word (*mkha' 'gro'i gsang tshig*) is their hidden speech, the code in which the secret teachings are kept, so that only one who holds its key can open them.

[^three-jewels]: The three jewels here are the three wish-fulfilling jewels of this cycle of teaching, named in the lines that follow; not the Three Jewels of all Buddhists, the Buddha, the Dharma and the Saṅgha.

[^wish-fulfilling-jewel]: A wish-fulfilling jewel (Sanskrit *cintāmaṇi*, Tibetan *yid bzhin nor bu*) is the gem of Indian legend that grants every wish. The teachings of the ḍākinīs' whispered lineage are kept as three such jewels, and this life belongs to the first of them, the jewel of the lineage.

[^introduction]: Introduction (*ngo sprod*) is the pointing-out instruction, in which a guru shows a disciple the nature of his own mind directly, as one introduces a person to someone he has not met.

[^dharma-body]: The dharma body (Sanskrit *dharmakāya*, Tibetan *chos sku*) is the first of the three bodies: awakened mind itself, empty and clear, without form, beyond anything that can be pointed to.

[^dream]: Dream (*rmi lam*) here is the yoga of dream: knowing that one dreams while dreaming, and training the mind in the dream. It is one of the six teachings later known as the six yogas of Nāropa, which Tilopa gathered from his four human gurus.

[^enjoyment-body]: The enjoyment body (Sanskrit *saṃbhogakāya*, Tibetan *longs sku*) is the second of the three bodies: the body of light, adorned with jewels, in which a buddha appears in a pure realm to beings of great realization. In the palace of 1.2 it is the rank of the ḍākinīs of the five families.

[^lineage]: A lineage (*brgyud pa*) is the unbroken line of teachers through which a teaching passes, each receiving it from the one before. "The jewel of the lineage" is the account of that line, beginning with Tilopa's life.

[^whispered-lineage]: The whispered lineage (Tibetan *snyan brgyud*, "ear lineage") is a line of teaching passed only by word of mouth, from a master's lips to a disciple's ear. The ḍākinīs' whispered lineage came from the ḍākinīs to Tilopa, and through Nāropa to Marpa.

[^clear-light]: Clear light (*'od gsal*), called "luminosity" in the rest of the book, is the bright, knowing nature of mind. The whispered lineage is that light, as the sun is light.

[^titan]: Rāhu is a titan (Sanskrit *asura*, Tibetan *lha min*, "not a god"), one of the jealous demigods at war with the gods, who in Indian myth swallows the sun and the moon in eclipse. The whispered lineage, the light of the sun, dreads him as the sun dreads eclipse.

[^blessing]: Blessing (*byin rlabs*) is the power of the buddhas, a guru or the ḍākinīs passed into a practitioner, which makes realization ripen; to bless is to give it.

[^instruction]: Instructions (*gdams ngag*) are a master's own practical teaching on how to practice, passed by word of mouth from teacher to disciple, as against the scriptures themselves.

[^dharma]: The Dharma (Sanskrit *dharma*, Tibetan *chos*) is the Buddha's teaching and the truth it teaches; "the holy Dharma" is the same.

[^experience-realization]: Experience and realization (*nyams rtogs*): experience is the passing states meditation brings, such as bliss, clarity and stillness; realization is lasting insight into the nature of mind. A master of the lineage has both.

[^compassion]: Compassion (*thugs rje*) is the wish that beings be free of suffering, and a master's will to act for them; in the teachings it is what moves a buddha to teach at all.

[^buddhahood]: A buddha (Tibetan *sangs rgyas*) is one fully awakened, who has woken from ignorance and brought every good quality to completion; buddhahood is that state. The Buddha of history is one buddha among countless buddhas.

[^one-life]: "In this one life and this one body" is the promise of the tantras: buddhahood won within a single lifetime, where the common path takes countless lives.

[^emanation-body]: The emanation body (Sanskrit *nirmāṇakāya*, Tibetan *sprul sku*) is the third of the three bodies: the form in which a buddha appears among ordinary beings, as a man, an animal or whatever will help them. Tilopa's life is told as the life of an emanation body.

[^false-notions]: False notions (*sgro 'dogs*, "laying on") are the ideas the mind lays over things that are not in them, and the doubts that come with them; an instruction that clears them away does it by hearing and reflection.

[^path-of-ripening]: The path of ripening (*smin lam*) is empowerment, which ripens a disciple's mind so that the practices can take root.

[^secretly]: Outer, inner and secret are the three levels at which the tantras give a teaching, each deeper and more hidden than the one before.

[^path-of-freedom]: The path of freedom (*grol lam*) is the instructions practiced after empowerment, which bring freedom; in this lineage, above all those that point out the nature of mind. The two together are "ripening and freeing".

[^wisdom-dakini]: The wisdom ḍākinī (*ye shes mkha' 'gro ma*) is a ḍākinī wholly awakened, the primordial wisdom of the buddhas in female form, as against the worldly ḍākinīs, who are spirits.

[^accomplished-ones]: The accomplished ones (Sanskrit *siddha*, Tibetan *grub thob*) are those who have won accomplishment by the practice of the tantras; the greatest of them, with Tilopa first in this lineage, are the mahāsiddhas, the great accomplished ones of India.

[^emanation]: An emanation (*sprul pa*) is a form an awakened being sends out or takes on by its power, a body, a person or a whole scene, real enough to act in the world. "An emanation of Cakrasaṃvara" is that deity appearing as Tilopa.

[^cakrasamvara]: Cakrasaṃvara (Tibetan *bde mchog*, "supreme bliss") is the deity of the tantra of that name and the chief deity of the ḍākinīs' whispered lineage: blue, with four faces and twelve arms, embracing his consort Vajravārāhī.

[^provisional]: In the provisional sense (*drang don*): taken at the level of ordinary appearances, as a teaching meant to lead beings on; here, his life told as though he were an ordinary man who needed gurus.

[^guru]: A guru (Tibetan *bla ma*, lama) is a spiritual teacher; in the tantras, the master who gives empowerment and instruction and is regarded as a buddha.

[^definitive]: In the definitive sense (*nges don*): taken as it finally is; here, that Tilopa, being a buddha, had no need of a human guru.

[^brahmin]: A Brahmin is a member of the priestly class of Indian society, the highest of the four, keepers of the Vedas and their rites. Tilopa is born into a Brahmin family.

[^read-the-marks]: The marks are the signs of the body, its features and lines, from which an Indian reader of marks foretold a child's fate. A buddha's body bears thirty-two marks of a great being.

[^god]: A god (Sanskrit *deva*, Tibetan *lha*) in Buddhism is a being of the heavens, long-lived and happy but still mortal and still in saṃsāra, and no creator.

[^naga]: A nāga (Tibetan *klu*) is a serpent spirit of springs, lakes and the underworld, a keeper of treasures and teachings, quick to bring sickness when offended.

[^yaksa]: A yakṣa (Tibetan *gnod sbyin*) is a powerful spirit of wealth and of wild places, sometimes a protector of the Dharma, sometimes a danger.

[^with-the-marks]: "With the marks" (*mtshan ma can*): bearing the signs by which a ḍākinī is known, here old age and ugliness, a strange breath and gait; the women who come to the boy are ḍākinīs.

[^demon]: Demons are harmful spirits. In the teachings the worst demon is the mind's own clinging, and one who does not know a ḍākinī may take her for a demon.

[^prophecy]: A prophecy (*lung bstan*) is a foretelling by a buddha, a deity or a ḍākinī, often in verse, that sends a practitioner to where his teaching or his disciples wait. Tilopa's life moves from one prophecy to the next.

[^sal]: The Tibetan names the tree only as *sha ba*; the sal tree, sacred in India, is the likeliest reading. The same tree, "a league high", stands in the charnel ground of 1.4.3.

[^oddiyana]: Oḍḍiyāna (Tibetan *u rgyan*) is a land of ancient India, most often placed in the Swat valley of today's Pakistan, held to be the home of the ḍākinīs and the source of many tantras.

[^vajravarahi]: Vajravārāhī, "Vajra Sow" (Tibetan *rdo rje phag mo*), is the consort of Cakrasaṃvara, a red ḍākinī who dances with a small sow's head beside her own; she is often called the queen of the ḍākinīs.

[^pancapana]: The name is not explained. It is nearly the name of the market town in Bengal where, in the prophecy that follows, Tilopa is sent to serve Bharima.

[^tree-of-awakening]: The tree of awakening (*byang chub shing*) is the bodhi tree under which the Buddha awoke; here the place where the boy is to win his own awakening.

[^charnel-ground]: A charnel ground (*dur khrod*) is a place where the dead were left in the open for the birds and beasts. Feared and lonely, it is where the yogins of the tantras practiced, among the corpses and the ḍākinīs who gather there.

[^mahesvara]: Maheśvara, "the Great Lord", is the Hindu god Śiva. The great charnel grounds of the tantras are often said to have been his.

[^feast]: The wheel of the feast (Sanskrit *gaṇacakra*, Tibetan *tshogs kyi 'khor lo*) is the gathering of the tantras in which practitioners offer food and drink, meat and liquor among them, to the deities and enjoy them as the deities. The "master of your feast" in 1.4.1 is the one who leads it.

[^caryapa]: Caryāpa, "the one of conduct", is one of the mahāsiddhas of India; in this life he is one of Tilopa's four human gurus, who gave him the instruction on dream.

[^lawapa]: Lawapa, "the Blanket-Clad", is the mahāsiddha also known as Kambala, who wore nothing but a blanket. One of Tilopa's four human gurus, he gave him luminosity.

[^siddhi]: A siddhi (Tibetan *dngos grub*), an accomplishment, is a power won by practice. The common siddhis are such powers as flight and magic; the supreme siddhi is awakening itself, Mahāmudrā.

[^mahamudra]: Mahāmudrā, "the great seal" (Tibetan *phyag rgya chen po*), is the nature of mind, empty and clear, the seal set on all things, and the practice of resting in it. It is the heart of the teaching of Tilopa's lineage, the Kagyü.

[^luminosity]: Luminosity (*'od gsal*), the "clear light" of the opening song: the bright, knowing nature of mind, and the practice of recognizing it, above all in deep sleep and at death. It is one of the six yogas.

[^nagarjuna]: Nāgārjuna here is the tantric master of that name, author of works on the Guhyasamāja tantra, whom Tibetan tradition holds to be the great philosopher of the Middle Way. In this life he is one of Tilopa's four human gurus, who gave him the illusory body.

[^outcaste]: An outcaste (Sanskrit *mātaṅga*) stood outside the four classes of Indian society, below them all. A yogin who lives in an outcaste's hut has set purity and impurity aside.

[^yogin]: A yogin (Tibetan *rnal 'byor pa*) is one who practices yoga; here a practitioner of the tantras who lives apart, often wandering and unconventional.

[^discipline]: The yogin's discipline (Sanskrit *vrata*, Tibetan *brtul zhugs*) is the chosen, often shocking way of life of the tantric yogins, living in charnel grounds and acting like madmen to cut through convention and to test their realization.

[^gandharva]: A gandharva (Tibetan *dri za*, "eater of scents") is a musician of the gods' heavens; "the city of the gandharvas" is a mirage, the image of a thing that seems real and is not.

[^mandala]: A maṇḍala is either of two things. To offer a maṇḍala is to offer the whole universe, set out in heaps of grain or jewels on a plate, to a guru when asking for a teaching. The maṇḍala of a deity is its palace and the circle of deities around it, drawn or imagined, into which a disciple is led at empowerment.

[^guhyasamaja]: Guhyasamāja, "the Secret Assembly" (Tibetan *gsang ba 'dus pa*), is one of the oldest of the highest tantras, and its deity.

[^empowerment]: An empowerment (Sanskrit *abhiṣeka*, Tibetan *dbang*) is the rite in which a master leads a disciple into a deity's maṇḍala and gives him the leave and the power to practice its tantra.

[^tantra]: A tantra (Tibetan *rgyud*) is a scripture of the Vajrayāna, the esoteric Buddhism of India and Tibet, which teaches the practice of a deity and its maṇḍala. To explain a tantra is to teach it.

[^nature-of-mind]: The nature of mind (*sems kyi ngo bo*) is what mind is in itself, beneath its thoughts: empty, clear and aware. To see it is the aim of the pointing-out instruction and of Mahāmudrā.

[^hevajra]: Hevajra (Tibetan *kye'i rdo rje*) is a deity of the highest tantras and the tantra named after him, much taught by the mahāsiddhas.

[^luhipa]: Lūhipa, or Lūipa, "the Eater of Fish Guts", is counted first of the eighty-four mahāsiddhas and was a master of the Cakrasaṃvara tantra; "what Lūhipa meant" is his way of practicing it.

[^hundred]: The Perfection of Wisdom in a Hundred Thousand Lines, the longest of the Perfection of Wisdom sutras. Once the manuscript calls it the Eight Thousand, a shorter one, and four times the Hundred Thousand; the English keeps to the Hundred Thousand.

[^two-stages]: The two stages are the stage of generation, in which one imagines oneself as the deity and the world as its maṇḍala, and the stage of completion, in which one works with the channels and winds of the body and rests in the nature of mind. Together they are the whole practice of the highest tantras.

[^sugata]: The Sugatas, "the Well-Gone" (Tibetan *bde bar gshegs pa*), are the buddhas.

[^conduct]: Conduct (Sanskrit *caryā*, Tibetan *spyod pa*) is, in the tantras, the last phase of practice, in which a yogin who has gained realization lives it out in the world, freely and against convention, to bring it to perfection; "to bring the conduct to its end" is to complete it.

[^accomplishment]: Accomplishment is siddhi, a power won by practice; "the accomplishment that is close to the supreme Mahāmudrā", below, is close to awakening itself.

[^delighted]: That Tilopa and Bharima "delighted each other in the conduct of the secret mantra" means they practiced together as consorts: in the highest tantras union with a consort is a method of the stage of completion.

[^secret-mantra]: The secret mantra (*gsang sngags*) is the Tibetan name for the Vajrayāna, the path of the tantras, called secret because it is given only to those who have been empowered.

[^bone-ornaments]: The bone ornaments are the six ornaments of carved human bone, a crown, earrings, a necklace, bracelets, a girdle and ash, worn by tantric yogins and by the wrathful deities as signs of the charnel ground.

[^innate]: The innate (Sanskrit *sahaja*, Tibetan *lhan cig skyes pa*, "born together") is the awakened nature born with the mind itself and never apart from it; to realize it is the aim of Mahāmudrā.

[^freed]: To be freed (*grol ba*) is liberation: freedom from saṃsāra, or here at least the moment of realizing the nature of mind. Throughout the book Tilopa's words free those who hear them.

[^vajra-song]: A vajra song (Tibetan *rdo rje'i mgur*, Sanskrit *dohā*) is a song of realization sung on the spot by a master of the tantras. A vajra (*rdo rje*) is the diamond thunderbolt, in the tantras the emblem of what cannot be broken, and so of awakening; "vajra" before a word makes it of that kind.

[^brahma]: Brahmā is the creator god of Hindu myth, in Buddhism the lord of a high heaven; a voice like Brahmā's is deep and sweet, one of the marks of a buddha.

[^dependent-arising]: Dependent arising (Sanskrit *pratītyasamutpāda*, Tibetan *rten cing 'brel bar 'byung ba*) is the Buddha's teaching that all things arise only in dependence on causes and conditions; its "links" are the conditions that must come together.

[^primordial-wisdom]: Primordial wisdom (Sanskrit *jñāna*, Tibetan *ye shes*) is the knowing of the awakened mind, present from the beginning, beyond the thinking mind.

[^suchness]: Suchness (Sanskrit *tathatā*, Tibetan *de nyid*) is things just as they are, their true nature, emptiness.

[^signs]: Signs (*brda*) are the gestures, symbols and objects by which a master shows what words cannot say; so in 1.2 the wisdom ḍākinī answers Tilopa in signs.

[^kye-ho]: Kye ho! is a cry that calls the hearer and marks wonder, often opening a song of realization.

[^remedy]: A remedy (*gnyen po*) is a practice that counters a fault, as love counters anger and wisdom counters ignorance. Where all is the innate, the song says, there is no fault to counter.

[^grounds-paths]: The grounds and paths (*sa lam*) are the stages of the way to buddhahood, the ten grounds of the bodhisattva and the five paths, by which most teachings measure a practitioner's progress.

[^convention]: Convention (*kun rdzob*, Sanskrit *saṃvṛti*) is the relative level of truth, where words and signs work, as against the ultimate, which they cannot reach.

[^four-transmissions]: The four transmissions (*bka' babs bzhi*) are the four lines of teaching Tilopa received from his four human gurus, which joined became the core of what was later taught as the six yogas of Nāropa.

[^illusory-body]: The illusory body (*sgyu lus*) is the practice of seeing one's body and all things as an illusion, a dream or a reflection, and at its height a subtle body of light; one of the six yogas.

[^inner-heat]: Inner heat (Sanskrit *caṇḍālī*, Tibetan *gtum mo*) is the yoga of kindling a fire at the navel through the channels and winds, which melts into great bliss; the root of the six yogas, said to keep yogins warm in the snows.

[^pledge]: A pledge (Sanskrit *samaya*, Tibetan *dam tshig*) is a vow of the tantras, the bond between disciple, guru and deity made at empowerment. To keep it is to be "true to the pledge"; to break it brings ruin.

[^fortune]: Fortune (*skal ba*) is the readiness for a teaching that good deeds in past lives bring; one "with the fortune" is fit to receive it.

[^ladder]: The text does not say what the "pair of grass mice" are, a key or a charm; they open the gate.

[^reference]: Reference (*dmigs pa*) is the mind's fixing on an object as this or that; "where reference has ceased" the mind grasps at nothing.

[^non-duality]: Non-duality (*gnyis su med pa*) is the absence of two: of subject and object, of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, of bliss and emptiness.

[^five-families]: The five families are the five families of buddhas, each headed by a buddha, Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi, and each the pure form of one of the mind's poisons; the ḍākinīs of the five families belong to them.

[^dakini-of-action]: The ḍākinī of action (*las kyi mkha' 'gro ma*) is a worldly ḍākinī who carries out tasks, gives powers and punishes, as against the wisdom ḍākinī; here she keeps the gate.

[^devotion]: Devotion, longing for and trust in the guru and the teaching, is held in Tilopa's lineage to be what opens the mind to blessing.

[^discipline-of-awareness]: The discipline of awareness (*rig pa brtul zhugs*) is the yogin's discipline carried by realization: Tilopa overwhelms the ḍākinīs by resting unmoved in awareness, the knowing nature of mind.

[^kye-ma]: Kye ma! is a cry of sorrow or of wonder.

[^heroes]: Heroes and heroines (*dpa' bo*, *dpa' mo*; Sanskrit *ḍāka*, *ḍākinī*) are the male and female deities around Cakrasaṃvara in his maṇḍala, the retinue of the ḍākinī of wisdom here.

[^blessed-lady]: The Blessed Lady (*bcom ldan 'das ma*) is the female form of the title "Blessed One". As mother of all the buddhas the wisdom ḍākinī is wisdom itself, from which the buddhas are born, as the Perfection of Wisdom is called the mother of the buddhas.

[^view]: The view is one's understanding of how things are; with conduct, the way one lives it, the fruit it brings and the pledge that binds it, it makes the whole of a practitioner's way.

[^painted-card]: A painted card (*tsa ka li*) is a small painting on card of a deity or a symbol, shown at an empowerment to introduce a disciple to what it shows.

[^seed-syllable]: A seed syllable (*yig 'bru*) is a single syllable of Sanskrit, such as Hūṃ or Āḥ, from which a deity arises when it is imagined, and which holds its essence.

[^hand-emblem]: A hand emblem (*phyag mtshan*) is the object a deity holds, a vajra, a skull cup or a curved knife, which stands for its mind and its qualities.

[^emptiness]: Emptiness (Sanskrit *śūnyatā*, Tibetan *stong pa nyid*) is the lack of any fixed, independent being in anything; "appearance and emptiness" are things appearing while empty, never apart.

[^free-of-thought]: Thought here is conceptual thinking, the mind's naming and judging; to be free of thought is to rest in the nature of mind without it.

[^way-things-are]: The way things are (*gnas lugs*) is reality as it is, beyond how it appears.

[^ignorance]: Ignorance (Sanskrit *avidyā*, Tibetan *ma rig pa*) is not knowing the nature of mind and of things, the root of saṃsāra.

[^self-aware]: Self-aware (*rang rig*), self-arisen (*rang byung*) and self-illumined (*rang gsal*) describe wisdom: it knows itself with no one watching, arises of itself without a cause, and shines by its own light.

[^mind-itself]: Mind itself (*sems nyid*) is the nature of mind, as against the thoughts that pass through it.

[^self-freed]: Self-freed (*rang grol*): a thought or feeling freed by itself, dissolving as it arises with nothing done to it, when the nature of mind is known.

[^nature-of-things]: The nature of things (Sanskrit *dharmatā*, Tibetan *chos nyid*) is the reality of all things, emptiness, which the song makes one with mind itself and the dharma body.

[^blessed-one]: The Blessed One (Sanskrit *bhagavān*, Tibetan *bcom ldan 'das*) is a title of a buddha and of the deities of the tantras.

[^root-tantra]: The root tantra is the chief scripture of a cycle, here the root tantra of Cakrasaṃvara, in fifty-one chapters; the explanatory tantras are the scriptures that explain it.

[^generation]: The stage of generation (*bskyed rim*), the first of the two stages: one imagines oneself as the deity, one's speech as its mantra and the world as its maṇḍala.

[^essence]: "The essence" (*snying po*) is most likely the heart mantra, the core mantra of a deity, by which its speech is accomplished.

[^completion]: The stage of completion (*rdzogs rim*), the second of the two stages: the imagined deity dissolved, one works with the channels and winds of the subtle body and rests in the nature of mind, which is Mahāmudrā.

[^worthy-vessels]: Worthy vessels (*snod ldan*) are disciples fit to hold the teaching, as a sound pot holds what is poured into it.

[^nine-cycles]: The nine cycles of the bodiless ḍākinīs (*lus med mkha' 'gro'i chos skor dgu*) are nine teachings, one to each of the nine lines that follow, which Tilopa receives from ḍākinīs without bodies, out of the sky; they are a core of the whispered lineage.

[^channels-winds]: The channels and winds (*rtsa rlung*) are the subtle body of the tantras: the channels through which the winds, the subtle energies the mind rides on, move, and which yogins train to draw into the central channel.

[^awareness]: Awareness (*rig pa*) is the knowing, awake quality of mind, mind's nature recognized.

[^sacred-substance]: A sacred substance (*dam rdzas*) is a substance blessed in the rites of the tantras and taken as a pledge, such as consecrated pills or nectar.

[^one-taste]: One taste (*ro snyoms*, "making the taste the same") is the practice in which every experience, pleasant or painful, is tasted as the one nature of mind.

[^all-knowing]: The All-Knowing (*thams cad mkhyen pa*) is a buddha, who knows all. The Kagyü say Tilopa's own guru was the buddha Vajradhara.

[^heretic]: A heretic (*mu stegs pa*, Sanskrit *tīrthika*) is a follower of one of the non-Buddhist schools of India.

[^acts-no-fruit]: Acts (Sanskrit *karma*, Tibetan *las*) bear fruit in the Buddhist teaching: every act, good or bad, brings its result to the one who did it, in this life or another. One who says acts bear no fruit denies karma, and with it any reason for virtue.

[^order]: The other tellings of Tilopa's life read beside this one tame the same people in another order. The lives of the gurus of the whispered lineage in a later manuscript make the heretic the fifth and the beer-seller the seventh, and their beer-seller is tamed when Tilopa holds the sun in the sky until her credit falls due; Tsangnyön's *Gzhung 'brel*, in both manuscripts of the 1971 Leh reprint, makes the beer-seller follow the magician and has Tilopa come to her as a beggar; the life in Marpa's collected works (the Sertsuk edition, 2009) tames the magician, the beer-seller, a hunter, a heretic teacher, a sorcerer, the singer and a strong man.

[^siddha]: The siddhas are the accomplished ones, masters of the tantras who have won siddhi; the paṇḍitas are the scholars, the siddhas the yogins.

[^rite-of-the-ground]: The rite of the ground (*sa chog*) opens a great rite of the tantras: the site is asked of its spirits, purified and blessed before a maṇḍala is drawn on it.

[^kusali]: A kusāli is a yogin of simple practice, who eats, sleeps and meditates and does nothing more, as against the learned paṇḍitas.

[^e-ma]: E ma! is a cry of wonder and amazement.

[^ultimate]: The ultimate (*don dam*, Sanskrit *paramārtha*) is the ultimate truth, things as they finally are, beyond words, as against the conventional truth of everyday appearance.

[^beyond-thought]: Beyond thought (*bsam gyis mi khyab pa*), inconceivable: each taming closes with Tilopa speaking of the art of the one he tames as beyond thought, his seat, his things, his music, his killing, and all who hear are freed.

[^without-passing-away]: "Without passing away" (*'das grongs med par*): living still, never having died, as the tradition says of siddhas who won a body beyond death; each of those Tilopa tames lives on in a sacred place.

[^nalanda]: Nālandā was the great monastic university of northern India, the chief seat of Buddhist learning from the fifth century to the end of the twelfth; "Śrī", like "glorious", is a title of honor.

[^outsiders-insiders]: Outsiders and insiders (*phyi nang*): the non-Buddhists, who seek outside the mind, and the Buddhists, who look within.

[^three-realms]: The three realms (*khams gsum*) are the whole of saṃsāra: the realm of desire, where humans and the lower gods live, the realm of form and the formless realm of the higher gods.

[^vase]: To fill the vase with wind is vase breathing, a yoga in which the breath is held in the belly as water is held in a vase, the root practice of inner heat.

[^cool-grove]: The Cool Grove (Sanskrit *Śītavana*) is a great charnel ground near Bodh Gaya, where the Buddha awoke, the first of the eight great charnel grounds of India.

[^sin]: Sin (*sdig pa*) is an unwholesome act, which brings suffering to the one who does it; there is no god it offends. Its opposite is virtue.

[^man-of-illusion]: The man of illusion (*sgyu ma'i skyes bu*) is a stock image of the teachings: a man conjured by a magician, who seems to live and act and is empty, as in the end all beings are.

[^attahasa]: Aṭṭahāsa, "Loud Laughter", is one of the eight great charnel grounds of India.

[^magician]: The magician's question and the first lines of Tilopa's answer stand also in Tsangnyön's *Gzhung 'brel* (the Jatang manuscript, page 34): "sgyu ma'i skyes bu bsod … sems med phyir na sdig pa med", "the man of illusion … since he has no mind, there is no sin".

[^ifyou]: The life in Marpa's collected works (the Sertsuk edition) gathers lines from the songs of these tamings into one long song Tilopa sings to Nāropa: "if you thirst, drink the water of poison; / if you grieve, look at the city of the gandharvas; / if you sink, fill the vase with wind; / if you stray, chase the monkey and kill it; … / if you cling, go into the charnel ground".

[^singer]: The life in Marpa's collected works names the singer of Nagara "the Mute with the Spear", *mdung can lkug pa*.

[^fire-end-of-time]: The fire at the end of time (*dus mtha'i me*) is the fire that burns up the world at the end of an aeon.

[^wind-mind]: The wind-mind (*rlung sems*) is the mind and the subtle wind it rides, which in the tantras are never apart.

[^crown]: To draw the wind-mind out through the crown is transference, the yoga of sending the mind out through the top of the head at death into a pure realm.

[^raksasa]: Rākṣasas (*srin po*) are flesh-eating demons of Indian myth; their island is Laṅkā.

[^butcher]: The life in Marpa's collected works tells the butcher's story the same way: Tilopa hides the son and makes the meat in the copper pot appear as the son's flesh, and the butcher gives up killing.

[^carvaka]: A Cārvāka, or Lokāyata, is a follower of the materialist school of India, which held that only what is seen is real and denied rebirth and the fruit of acts.

[^virtue]: Virtue (*dge ba*) is a wholesome act, which brings happiness to the one who does it; virtue and sin are the two kinds of karma.

[^hell]: The hells (*dmyal ba*) are realms of torment, hot and cold, where beings are born by the force of their sins until it is spent; they are not eternal, and, as the song says, they are the mind's own making.

[^higher-realms]: The higher realms (*mtho ris*) are birth as a human or a god, the fruit of virtue.

[^victory-banner]: The victory banner (*rgyal mtshan*) is one of the eight auspicious symbols, the banner of the Dharma's victory over delusion.

[^discursive-thought]: Discursive thought is the stream of concepts in the mind. That it "is the emanation body" is the view of Mahāmudrā: thoughts are the play of the nature of mind, not something to be cut away.

[^sriparvata]: Śrīparvata, "the Glorious Mountain", in the south of India, is a mountain of the siddhas, where Nāgārjuna is said to have spent his last years.

[^sorcerer]: The Tibetan of this passage is hard, and the English gives its likeliest sense: the two are matched in killing; Tilopa takes up all who died but some, the sorcerer's women among them, and offers to raise those too if the sorcerer gives up his magic.

[^samsara]: Saṃsāra, "wandering" (Tibetan *'khor ba*), is the round of birth and death in which beings circle, driven by their acts, until they are freed.

[^abbot-master]: The abbot and master (*mkhan slob*) are the two preceptors who give a monk his ordination; here Tilopa's own uncle and mother.

[^three-wheels]: The three wheels of a monk's work are study, meditation and work for the community.

[^provost]: The provost (*dge skos*) is the monk who keeps discipline in a monastery and calls its assembly.

[^birds]: The word for what he killed is not in the dictionaries; that they fly away at the end of the scene makes them small birds.

[^kalpa]: A kalpa (Tibetan *bskal pa*) is an aeon, the immense span of a world's forming, lasting and ending.

[^buddha-fields]: A buddha field (*zhing khams*) is the pure realm of a buddha, where he teaches.

[^aryadeva]: Āryadeva was Nāgārjuna's disciple and with him a founder of the Middle Way; tantric tradition knows a master of the same name among Nāgārjuna's disciples.

[^thousand-buddhas]: The thousand buddhas are those who appear in this "fortunate aeon"; the Buddha Śākyamuni is the fourth of them.

[^yoke]: To keep the eyes a yoke's length ahead, about six feet, and set the feet down slowly is how the monastic rule asks a monk to walk on his alms round.

[^giving-receiving]: The blessings of giving and receiving (*sbyin len gyi shis pa*) are the verses a monk recites over the alms he is given, for the good of the giver.

[^three-times]: The three times are past, present and future.

[^colored-powders]: A maṇḍala of colored powders is drawn on the ground in powdered stone for an empowerment and swept away when the rite is done; Tilopa raises his in the sky.

[^mahamaya]: Mahāmāyā, "Great Illusion", is a deity of the highest tantras and the tantra named after it.

[^aggregates-elements]: The aggregates (*phung po*) are the five heaps that make a person, form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness; the elements (*khams*) are earth, water, fire, wind and space. The tantras see both as deities.

[^vajrabhairava]: Vajrabhairava, "Vajra Terror" (Tibetan *rdo rje 'jigs byed*), is a wrathful deity of the highest tantras with the head of a buffalo, the destroyer of death.

[^mangalam]: Maṅgalam, Sanskrit for "good fortune", closes many Tibetan books as a blessing.

[^colophon]: Marpa writes at Drowolung, his seat in Lhodrak, for his son Dode, Darma Dode.
